# 1. Metapatterns

> Denys Poltorak, *Architectural Metapatterns* v1.2 (05-2026), pp. 9–13

The opening chapter states the central idea of the book: **architectural patterns can be
classified**, and the axis to classify them along is *structure*.

The problem it starts from — there are literally thousands of published patterns, nobody knows
them all, and old ones get rebranded, reinvented, or forgotten. To bring order, we need a subset
of patterns that is:

- **Important** enough to matter to the majority of programmers
- **Small** enough to fit in one's memory or in a book
- **Complete** enough to assure nothing crucial is missed

<br/>

## 1.1 Architectural patterns

`[POSA1]` defines three categories of patterns:

|Category|Scope|
|:---|:---|
|**Architectural patterns**|Overall structure of a system and the functions of its components|
|**Design patterns**|Relations between objects|
|**Idioms**|Abstractions on top of a given programming language|

Architectural patterns are chosen as the subject of study, and they satisfy two of the three
requirements up front:

- **Importance (point 1)** — architectural patterns are important *by definition*, since
  "architecture is about the important stuff."
- **Completeness (point 3)** — any system has an internal structure, and describing it usually
  boils down to a composition of two or three well-known architectural patterns. Because
  architectures are advertised loudly (either as the latest fashion or as a proud novelty), we can
  expect to have heard of nearly all the architectures used in practice.

Point 2 (small size) is not yet satisfied — that is what the rest of the chapter builds toward.

<br/>

## 1.2 Design space

*Design space* `[POSA1, POSA5]` is a model that allocates **one dimension per design choice**
made while architecting a system, so it contains every possible way a system could be designed.

The trouble: it is multidimensional, possibly infinite, and its dimensions differ from system to
system.

The workaround is to **project** the design space onto 2 or 3 dimensions that humans are
comfortable with. Projection loses information — and counterintuitively **that loss is exactly what
we want**: similar architectures that differ only in minor details become *identical* once the
dimensions they differ in disappear.

So the task becomes: find the 2–3 most important dimensions that apply equally well to every
architectural pattern.

<br/>

## 1.3 Structure determines architecture

Systems tend to have an internal structure; those that don't are derogatively called *Big Balls of
Mud*. Structure is about `components, their roles and interactions`.

The naming of architectural styles is itself evidence that structure is what matters — *Layers* and
*Pipeline* are named directly after their structures, while others such as *Event-Driven
Architecture* highlight a structural aspect.

**Metapatterns** — `clusters of patterns of similar structure` — are not a new idea. They were
defined shortly after the first collections of design patterns appeared, but never made a lasting
impact. The author's diagnosis: the approach was applied **prematurely to the `[GoF]` patterns**,
which are a rather random and incomplete subset of design patterns, so the result was an
overgeneralization.

The plan for this book:

1. Plot the structures of all encountered architectural patterns
2. Group patterns of identical structure into metapatterns
3. Draw the relations between the metapatterns
4. Show how a system's structure determines its properties

This still requires a **universal system of coordinates**, since structural diagrams are drawn
differently from source to source and cannot otherwise be compared.

<br/>

## 1.4 The system of coordinates

All architectural patterns share the same scope — the system as a whole — so three dimensions
suffice:

|#|Dimension|Meaning|Drawn|
|:---:|:---|:---|:---|
|1|**Abstractness**|High-level use cases vs. low-level details. One abstract operation unrolls into many lower-level ones (Python → C runtime → assembly drivers; orchestrators → service APIs → SQL → disk ops)|Vertically, higher-level on top|
|2|**Subdomain**|Any complex system manages multiple subdomains (an OS deals with video card, HDD, TCP/IP; an enterprise has departments)|Horizontally|
|3|**Sharding**|Multiple deployed instances of a module, when that is an integral part of the architecture|Diagonally|

The chapter introduces this notation with a structural diagram of **CQRS** (adapted from Udi
Dahan): *Frontend* replicas → *Reverse Proxy* → *Backend* (containing *Domain Model* + *ORM*)
→ *OLTP DB*, with an event stream feeding a *Cache* → *OLAP DB* on the read side.

Component coloring used throughout the book:

|Color|Meaning|
|:---|:---|
|🟩 Green|Use cases (application logic)|
|🟦 Blue|Business rules (domain logic)|
|⬜ White|Generic code|
|⬛ Gray|Data|

Arrow notation: asynchronous message, optional message, event stream, method call or RPC, and
2-way communication.

> **Caveat on abstractness** — abstractness is usually *inverse* to the distance to the system's
> clients: a GUI is highly abstract and is what users touch, while the far end hosts device drivers
> speaking in bits and registers. Reality is messier (drawing a UI window still needs the graphics
> driver deep in the OS, and several routing/proxy layers sit between a web page and the
> server-side logic). Those intermediate layers are not highly abstract, but are still drawn near
> the top `to keep the diagrams simple and stupid`.

<br/>

## 1.5 Map and reduce

With generic coordinates in hand, the method is:

1. **Map** the set of architectural patterns into the coordinate system — reducing the
   multidimensional design space down to the few dimensions of a structural diagram
2. **Filter out** the minor details
3. The ~100 published patterns then yield a *score* of **topologies** — clusters of geometrically
   equivalent diagrams — simply because there are very few simple systems one can draw on a plane
   before repeating oneself

Each topology represents an **architectural metapattern**: `a generalization of architectural
patterns of similar structure and function`.

The three requirements are now all met:

|Requirement|How it is satisfied|
|:---|:---|
|**Importance**|Proven earlier — architectural patterns are important by definition|
|**Small size**|Granted by the existence of only a few simple 2D/3D shapes (topologies)|
|**Completeness**|The geometrical approach makes blank spaces (possible geometries with no known patterns) obvious, plus the sample of classified patterns is large|

<br/>

## 1.6 An example of metapatterns

Consider one structure: **two (or more) high-level modules that communicate with/via a single
lower-level module.**

Which patterns match it?

- **Middleware** — software providing the means of communication between other components
- **Shared Database** — a space for other components to store and exchange data
- **Model-View-Controller** — platform-agnostic business logic with customized input and output

At first this looks like the idea backfired: one structure, three distinct patterns. But:

- The first two are genuinely **related** — both implement indirect communication, and the
  distinction fades in practice (a Middleware may have persistent message storage; a table in a
  Shared Database may be used to orchestrate services).
- The third is **very different**, and the reason is *where the business logic sits*: in MVC the bulk
  of the code resides in the **lower** layer, leaving the upper components a minor role.

And each of the three turns out to be the seed of a distinct cluster:

|Pattern|Cluster it belongs to|
|:---|:---|
|**Middleware**|aka *(Message) Broker* `[POSA1, POSA4, EIP, MP]`; integral to *Message Bus* `[EIP]`, *Service Mesh* `[FSA]`, *Event Mediator* `[FSA]`, *Enterprise Service Bus* `[FSA]`, *Space-Based Architecture* `[SAP, FSA]`|
|**Shared Database**|a kind of *Shared Repository* `[POSA4]` (*Shared Memory*, *Shared File System*); foundation for *Blackboard* `[POSA1, POSA4]`, *Space-Based Architecture* `[SAP, FSA]`, *Service-Based Architecture* `[FSA]`|
|**Model-View-Controller**|`[POSA1, POSA4]` a special kind of *Hexagonal Architecture* (aka *Ports and Adapters*, *Onion Architecture*, *Clean Architecture*), itself derived from *Plugins* `[PEAA]` (*Addons*, *Plug-In Architecture* `[FSA]`, or the misnomer *Microkernel Architecture* `[SAP, FSA]`)|

Touching a **single topology** revealed a web of some **twenty pattern names** — evidence that the
whole fabric, a *pattern language* `[GoF, POSA1, POSA2, POSA5]`, can be explored this way.

**Three lessons:**

1. The **distribution of business logic** is a crucial aspect of topologies
2. Metapatterns are **interrelated in multiple ways**, forming a pattern language
3. Each metapattern **includes several well-established patterns**

<br/>

## 1.7 What does that mean

Chemistry has the periodic table. Biology has the tree of life. This book strives to build
something of that kind for software and systems architecture.

The anticipated objection — *"Chemistry and biology are empirical sciences while software
architecture isn't!"* — is left hanging as a question: **Is it?**

<br/>

## Summary

|Step|Idea|
|:---:|:---|
|1|There are too many patterns; classify a subset that is important, small, and complete|
|2|Architectural patterns are that subset (important by definition, and well documented)|
|3|Design space has too many dimensions → project it, and welcome the information loss|
|4|Project onto **abstractness / subdomain / sharding**|
|5|Geometrically equivalent diagrams cluster into **topologies**|
|6|Each topology = one **metapattern**, generalizing patterns of similar structure and function|
|7|Metapatterns interlink into a pattern language — the goal is a "periodic table" of architecture|
